If you completed two years of a degree and stopped, those credits do not disappear. The papers you passed stay on your record at that university, and another recognized university can choose to count them towards its own programme, so that you finish what remains instead of starting again. What you cannot do is carry them across automatically. The credits are yours, but their value in a new programme is decided by the university receiving them.

That one distinction clears up most of the confusion we hear from people who left a degree partway through because a job abroad came up, the fees became impossible or the family situation changed. This article explains the idea. If you want the practical procedure, our [guide to credit transfer in Kerala](/blog/credit-transfer-degree-kerala) covers the application side, and it is the better read once you know the concept applies to you.

## What actually happens to your credits when you stop studying?

Nothing happens to them, in the sense that a passed paper stays passed. It helps to think of a credit as passing through three separate stages, because people often assume that reaching the first means reaching the third.

- **Earned.** You passed the paper. Your marksheet is the proof, and the award belongs to the university that examined you.

- **Recorded.** The credit sits in a record someone else can check. For recent study this may be the Academic Bank of Credits. For older study it is usually only your marksheets and the university's own registers.

- **Accepted.** A second university has compared the paper with its own syllabus and agreed to count it. Until this happens, the credit has no effect on your new degree.

Stopping your studies leaves you at the first or second stage. Credit transfer is the process of reaching the third. Note what it does not do: your old university does not award you anything further, and your unfinished programme does not turn into a certificate by itself. Some programmes have an exit credential, which is a different question.

## Do credits from an unfinished degree expire?

There is no simple yes or no, because a passed paper does not stop being passed, but a university may decline to count it after a certain time. Each receiving university sets its own limit on how old credits can be, and the limit can differ by programme. No single number holds across universities, so ask for the limit that applies to the programme you want.

Separately, your old university has its own limit on how long you may stay registered and clear pending papers there. These are two different clocks. You might be too late to finish at your old college and still be in time to transfer the papers you passed, or the reverse. If you are weighing those routes, our piece on [ways to complete a discontinued degree](/blog/complete-discontinued-degree-kerala) sets them side by side.

## Which credits usually count, and which do not?

Passed papers from a recognized university in the same or a closely related subject usually have the best chance of counting. These are patterns, not promises.

| Your earlier study | Likely treatment | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Passed papers, same subject (B.Com to B.Com) | Often counted | The syllabus maps closely |
| Language and foundation papers | Often counted | Most programmes have an equivalent |
| Passed papers, related subject (BBA to B.Com) | Counted in part | Only papers with matching content map |
| Uncleared or backlog papers | Not counted | There is no pass, so no credit exists |
| Institution not recognized when you attended | Generally not counted | The earlier award has no standing to transfer |
| Very different field (engineering to social work) | Little or nothing | Few papers match, even if every one was passed |

Two points catch people out. Attendance is not credit: a year spent in college without passing any papers gives a university nothing to count. And recognition is judged for the period when you studied, so a college's reputation today does not settle it.

## How much of a degree can credit transfer save?

It can save a large part of a degree or very little, and you only know after a paper-by-paper assessment. Years completed are a poor guide, because the receiving university counts matching papers, not time spent.

Here is an illustrative composite, not a real student. Someone completes four semesters of a three-year B.Com and stops. He passed most of those papers, but two remain uncleared. He assumes he has "one year left". The assessment may show three things:

- The papers he passed that match the new syllabus are counted.

- The two uncleared papers are not counted, so he studies those subjects again.

- A few papers he did pass cover content the new syllabus arranges differently, so they may not map, and he studies the equivalents.

His answer could be "the final year plus some extra papers", which is still far better than three years, but it is not the figure he had in his head. The receiving university will also have a minimum period you must study with it, whatever you bring across, because it is the one awarding the degree.

Fees follow the same logic. Your fee should reflect what you actually have left to study, and you should see that in writing, with the papers listed, before you pay anything.

## Is a degree completed through credit transfer valid?

Yes, a degree completed this way is an ordinary degree of the university that awards it, provided that university and programme are properly recognized. Its validity for a particular purpose depends on the same three things as any other distance or online degree: the university's recognition, the programme's DEB entitlement for your admission year, and the wording of the job notification you are applying under.

Ask how the earlier study will appear on your new marksheets, so that you can explain it if an employer or verification officer asks. Keep your old marksheets permanently. They are the evidence for part of your degree.

## The mistakes that cost returning students the most

- **Assuming that the Academic Bank of Credits has done the work.** The bank stores credits. It does not make a university accept them, and study from some years ago may never have been uploaded. Our [explainer on the ABC ID](/blog/academic-bank-of-credits-abc-id-explained) shows how to check.

- **Accepting a promise made over the phone.** "You can join the final year" means little without the decision on paper, naming the programme, the entry point and the papers accepted.

- **Assuming that lateral entry and credit transfer are the same.** One is a block decision on a qualification, the other is itemised by paper. If you hold a diploma, ask which of the two the university is actually offering.

- **Bringing only the final marksheet.** The assessment is made on paper, so gather every semester marksheet you hold, any transfer or migration certificate, your 12th certificate and the syllabus if you still have it. Where a marksheet is lost, the old university can usually issue a duplicate, so request it early.
