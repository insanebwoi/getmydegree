Yes. A gap of ten years, or twenty, does not by itself stop you from joining a degree programme. Universities admitting students to distance and online programmes look at whether you hold the qualifying examination, which is a pass in the 12th from a recognized board for a bachelor's degree and a bachelor's degree for a master's. What the gap changes is the paperwork, and, if you started a degree long ago, whether any of that earlier study can still be counted.

## What the gap actually affects

People expect the gap to be the problem. Usually it is not. The questions that matter are more practical.

- Can you produce the original certificates and marksheets for your qualifying examination, or obtain duplicates.

- Is your name, and your date of birth, written the same way on every document.

- Did you study part of a degree before, and if so, how long ago and at which university.

If you only ever completed Plus Two and are starting a degree fresh, the first two are the whole job. If you left a degree half-way, the third question decides whether you start from semester one or somewhere later.

## The gap certificate or affidavit

Some universities ask a student with a long break in study to explain it. This is usually done with a gap affidavit: a short sworn statement, typically on stamp paper and attested by a notary, saying what you were doing during the years you were not studying, such as working, running a household or living abroad. Others do not ask for one at all.

It is not a test and it is not held against you. It simply closes a question in your file. Write it truthfully and plainly. If you were working, say so and give the years; if you were looking after family, say that. The university's admission form or your counsellor will tell you whether one is needed and in what format, so do not prepare one until you know the university requires it.

## Old marksheets and documents

A decade is long enough for certificates to be lost in a house move, damaged by monsoon damp or left with a relative in another district. Start collecting them before you start choosing a programme.

- Your 10th certificate, which is the usual proof of name and date of birth.

- Your Plus Two or equivalent certificate and marksheet. Kerala HSE, VHSE and other recognized boards and open schooling systems all issue duplicates through their own procedures.

- For a postgraduate admission, your degree certificate, consolidated marksheet and, if the university asks, a migration certificate.

- An identity proof whose name matches the certificates.

If anything is missing, apply for the duplicate from the board or university that issued it. This can take weeks, so it is the first thing to start. If your name was spelled differently on one document, or changed after marriage, sort out the supporting proof now rather than at the point a university returns your application.

## If you studied part of a degree before

This is where the long gap genuinely matters, and where you should be careful about anyone who promises a quick answer.

Credit transfer means a university accepts some of the papers you passed elsewhere and lets you join at a later semester. The receiving university decides this under its own rules, and those rules commonly look at how old the credits are. Many set a limit on how many years can have passed since you earned them. A pass from a decade ago, under a syllabus that has since been revised twice, may simply not be accepted.

There is a second, separate limit. Every university sets a maximum period within which a programme must be completed after registration. If that window has closed at your original university, you usually cannot go back and finish there either; the registration has lapsed.

The Academic Bank of Credits now stores credits digitally against an APAAR ID, and credits recorded there can be carried between institutions registered with it, subject to the receiving university's rules and the validity period the scheme sets for credits. Study completed many years ago, before the scheme existed, will generally not be in ABC at all, so it is assessed from your old marksheets instead. Our guide to the [Academic Bank of Credits and the APAAR ID](/blog/academic-bank-of-credits-abc-id-explained) explains how it works for newer credits.

So the honest position is this. After a long gap, old credits may transfer, partly transfer, or not transfer at all, and it depends entirely on the university. Do not pay anyone who tells you otherwise before they have seen your marksheets and named the receiving university's rule. Our [credit transfer assessment](/credit-transfer) is where we look at this case by case.

## Starting fresh is often the cleaner route

When old credits are not accepted, a fresh admission is not a defeat. It gives you a full, regular record at one university, with no gaps to explain at verification. For many people returning after ten years, it is also simply easier: the syllabus is current, and they are not trying to remember material from a different decade.

If you are weighing the options, our comparison of [credit transfer, lateral entry and fresh admission](/blog/credit-transfer-vs-lateral-entry) sets out which tends to fit which situation.

## Will the degree be valid after a gap

The gap has no bearing on validity. What matters is the same for everyone. The university must be recognized by the UGC, the programme must carry Distance Education Bureau entitlement for the mode and the academic year you are admitted in, and, for a government post, the notification itself must accept a degree earned that way.

That last point is not a formality in Kerala. In *Kerala Public Service Commission v. Vaisakh C.L.* (January 2026), the Kerala High Court held that where a post's rules or notification require a qualification through a regular course of study, a distance degree does not satisfy it. Read the wording of any notification you are aiming at. Our guide on [whether a distance degree is valid for government jobs](/blog/is-a-distance-degree-valid-for-government-jobs) covers this in more detail.

## Returning to study after years away

The academic side is usually less of a worry than people expect. Adults who come back after a long break tend to be more organised than they were at eighteen, and they know why they are there. The adjustment is about routine: finding fixed hours, getting used to reading again, and learning the examination pattern. Give yourself the first month to settle before judging how it is going.

## Practical next steps

- Gather your 10th, Plus Two and any degree documents now, and apply for duplicates of anything missing.

- Check that your name and date of birth match across all of them.

- If you studied part of a degree, find every marksheet you have, with the year of each examination.

- Decide what the degree is for: a promotion, a government notification, a job abroad, or your own satisfaction. That decides the programme and the checks.

- Bring all of it to a counsellor, who will tell you whether a gap affidavit is needed, whether any earlier credits are worth assessing, and the full fee in writing before you pay anything.
