You can complete a master's degree while working full-time by choosing an online or distance programme that fits your work pattern, confirming your bachelor's degree qualifies you, and accepting that two years of your evenings and weekends will shrink. Whether you should do it is a separate question. It is usually worth it when a specific promotion, role or further study requires the degree, and much less so when you simply feel you ought to have one.

So decide that first, then work out how to fit it in. The study habits themselves are covered in our guide to [studying while working full-time](/blog/studying-while-working-full-time). This article deals with the decision and the arrangement around it.

## Is a master's degree worth it while working full-time?

It is worth it if you can name what it will unlock, and doubtful if you cannot.

Sort your reason into one of these groups.

- **A written requirement.** Your employer's promotion policy, a recruitment notification or a doctoral programme asks for a postgraduate degree. This is the strongest reason, as the degree is the thing standing in the way.

- **A planned change of function.** You want to move from accounts to operations, or from support to a technical lead role, and the degree teaches what you lack. This is a good reason if you have checked that people in that role do hold such a qualification.

- **Protection for later.** You want to be eligible when an opening comes up, or for a job abroad. This is reasonable, but test it: look at real listings for the role.

- **A general sense of falling behind.** This is the weakest reason. It rarely carries anyone through two years of tired evenings. Short courses may do more for you at less cost. Our piece on [micro-credentials and specialisations](/blog/micro-credentials-and-specializations) helps you tell.

Then count the real cost, which has three parts: the fee, the time taken from family and rest, and the risk that a heavy year at work delays you. We cannot tell you what the degree will add to your pay, and you should distrust anyone who gives you a figure. Instead, ask your HR section whether the qualification changes your grade or eligibility, and ask two people in the role you want whether the degree mattered in getting it.

## Can I do a master's without leaving my job?

Yes, you can, because online and distance modes are designed around not attending daily classes.

The formats differ in ways that matter for your particular job.

| Format | How it runs | Suits |
|---|---|---|
| Self-paced online | Recorded lectures, assessment at fixed points | Irregular or shift work; needs the most self-discipline |
| Live weekend or evening online | Fixed timetable with a cohort | Predictable weeks; people who need a scheduled obligation |
| Open and distance learning | Study material, periodic contact sessions, semester examinations | People with limited screen time or patchy internet |

Which suits you depends on your week. As illustrative examples, not real individuals: a nurse on rotating shifts in Malappuram cannot promise Saturday at ten, so a live-session programme will fail her, while a bank employee with a settled weekday routine may do better with that fixed session because it gives her week a structure. For the difference in detail, see [online and distance modes compared](/blog/online-vs-distance-degree-modes).

The mode also has a legal side. UGC's 2020 regulations treat online and distance degrees from recognized institutions with DEB entitlement as equivalent to conventional ones. But validity for your purpose always depends on three things: the university's recognition, the programme's DEB entitlement for your admission year, and the wording of the job notification. If a post requires a "regular course of study", a distance degree will not meet it, as the Kerala High Court held in January 2026. Check before you commit, particularly if a government post is the reason you are studying.

## How many hours a week does a master's take when you have a job?

Plan on roughly eight to ten hours a week as a working figure, unevenly spread, though this is our rough guide and not a university rule.

Assignment weeks and the fortnight before examinations will take considerably more. Other weeks will take less than you expect. What matters is whether you can find that time in a bad week.

Try this before enrolling. For two ordinary weeks, write down what you actually do from waking to sleeping. Include the commute, family duties, overtime and the hours you are simply too tired to read. Then mark where study could go. An illustrative example, not a real person's record: a sales executive working six days a week finds Tuesday and Thursday evenings give him about two hours each, and Sunday morning gives him four. That is eight hours, with nothing to spare in a target-closing week. He now knows he has to plan his month-end around the semester calendar or talk to his manager. It is better to learn that now than in the first semester.

If the test shows you have nowhere near enough time, consider what could give for two years, or wait for a calmer year. A delayed start costs less than an abandoned programme.

## What to arrange before the semester starts

Eligibility comes first. A master's programme requires a completed bachelor's degree from a recognized university. Some universities set a minimum aggregate, and some PG programmes want a related discipline, though many MBA programmes accept any. If your bachelor's degree has a pending paper, resolve that before anything else. It is the one problem nobody can work around for you, and [degree with supplementary papers](/blog/degree-with-supplementary-papers) explains the route.

Next, sort out the people and the money.

- **Talk to your employer if the degree affects your job.** Ask whether they recognize it, whether it needs permission under your service rules, and whether leave can be arranged for examination dates. Government employees in particular should check for any required permission first.

- **Agree on the difficult weeks at home.** A plan for deadline weeks made with your family before the semester works better than one negotiated during it.

- **Get the whole fee in writing.** Include examination and any project charges. We give the breakdown in writing before you pay, and our instalment plans are monthly, with no interest and no processing fee.

- **Choose a specialisation that points somewhere.** At PG level it is the part employers read. If you do not yet know where you are heading, a general management or commerce programme keeps more doors open than a narrow one aimed at a role you may not want.

## The mistakes that end programmes

- **Starting in your employer's busiest season.** If your first examinations fall in your worst month, the first semester sets a bad pattern. Ask about the intake timing.

- **Taking a harder degree than you need.** If the goal is a promotion on any PG degree, this is not the time to discover a difficult quantitative subject.

- **Treating a missed semester as the end.** Ask at the start what the university allows if you need to defer or repeat, and what the maximum time to finish is.

- **Keeping it quiet.** If nobody knows you are studying, nobody can cover for you in examination week or ask how it is going.
