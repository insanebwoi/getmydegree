People rarely abandon a degree because the material was too hard. They abandon it because a bad month became a bad semester and re-entry felt impossible. Flexibility is the reason people choose this route and the reason some do not finish: when nothing is scheduled, nothing is protected, and the degree loses every negotiation against a deadline at work. Nearly all of the difference is in how the time is arranged.

## Fix the hours before the semester starts

Six to ten hours a week is the realistic figure for most programmes. Decide which hours, specifically, and put them in the same place every week. Two weekday evenings and one longer weekend block is the pattern that survives longest.

The point of fixing them in advance is that you stop making the decision. A study slot you negotiate with yourself each week is one you will lose to a bad day at work. Treat it like a shift, and defend it the way you would defend a work meeting.

## Work backwards from the assessment dates

- Get the semester calendar on day one and write every submission and examination date into the same calendar you use for work.

- Look for collisions with your known busy periods, such as a year-end close or a festival rush, and deal with them now, while there is room to move.

- Set your own deadline five days before each real one. That gap absorbs the week work explodes.

- Break each assignment into reading, drafting and revising, and schedule those separately.

A deadline you meet three days early costs the same hours as one you meet at midnight, and costs nothing in stress.

## Study in a way that suits the assessment

If the programme is assessed by written examination, practise writing answers under time rather than re-reading material. If it is assessed by assignments, start each one the week it is set and leave it open, rather than treating it as a single evening's work.

Re-reading feels productive and is the weakest use of the hours. Answering questions from memory is uncomfortable, and it is what actually works.

## Use the commute and the dead time

Reading and lecture material work well in fragments: a bus ride, a lunch break, twenty minutes before bed. Save the continuous blocks for writing, which does not survive interruption. Students routinely cover most of their reading without ever sitting at a desk for it.

## Tell two people

Tell someone at home and someone at work that you are studying. The first protects your evenings, and most failures at this stage are domestic rather than academic. The second means a manager knows why you left at six on a Thursday. You are not asking permission, only removing the friction of explaining it every week.

## Use your work

Where assignments allow a real example, use one from your job. It is faster to write, it is better than an invented case, and it makes the degree immediately useful rather than parallel to your working life.

## Plan for the bad month

There will be one: a project deadline, an illness, a wedding. Know in advance what the university's rules are for a deferred examination, a re-sit or a break in study, and what they cost. Knowing the mechanism exists is what makes a bad month survivable rather than terminal.

If you are going to miss something, contact your counsellor and the university that week, before the deadline rather than after. Extensions and rescheduled submissions are routine when asked for in time; the gap between a problem and asking about it is what turns one paper into a lost semester.

> The people who finish are not the ones with more free time. They are the ones who asked for help in week three instead of week ten.
