You finish a degree while working full time by fixing your study hours in advance, planning backwards from assessment dates, and asking for help the moment a bad week threatens to become a bad semester. People rarely abandon a degree because the material was too hard. They abandon it because a bad month became a bad semester and re-entry felt impossible. Flexibility is the reason people choose this route and the reason some do not finish: when nothing is scheduled, nothing is protected, and the degree loses every negotiation against a deadline at work. Nearly all of the difference is in how the time is arranged.

## How many hours a week should I study while working full time?

Six to ten hours a week is the realistic figure for most programmes, in our experience. It is a planning figure rather than a university rule, so adjust it once you have seen your first semester's workload.

Decide which hours, specifically, and put them in the same place every week. Two weekday evenings and one longer weekend block is the pattern that survives longest.

The point of fixing them in advance is that you stop making the decision. A study slot you negotiate with yourself each week is one you will lose to a bad day at work. Treat it like a shift, and defend it the way you would defend a work meeting.

Here is what that can look like for someone in a nine-to-six job, as an example to adapt:

| Slot | Time | Best used for |
|---|---|---|
| Tuesday evening | 90 minutes | New material, lectures, notes |
| Thursday evening | 90 minutes | Practice questions from memory |
| Sunday morning | 3 hours | Assignment writing, the week's hardest paper |
| Commute or lunch | As available | Reading, recorded lectures |

Add a fixed minimum for your worst weeks: perhaps 30 minutes on two days. A small minimum you keep is what stops one missed week turning into a habit of missing. If your roster changes every week, this fixed pattern will not hold, and our notes for [healthcare and shift workers](/blog/degrees-for-healthcare-and-shift-workers) are a better starting point.

## How do I plan a semester around my job?

Plan the semester by working backwards from every assessment date, starting on the first day you get the calendar.

- Get the semester calendar on day one and write every submission and examination date into the same calendar you use for work.

- Look for collisions with your known busy periods, such as a year-end close or a festival rush, and deal with them now, while there is room to move. In Kerala retail and hospitality, Onam and the Ramzan and Christmas seasons are obvious ones. Apply for leave around examination dates early, before colleagues do.

- Set your own deadline five days before each real one. That gap absorbs the week work explodes.

- Break each assignment into reading, drafting and revising, and schedule those separately.

A deadline you meet three days early costs the same hours as one you meet at midnight, and costs nothing in stress.

At the end of each month, spend ten minutes checking three things: what is due next, what you have fallen behind on, and whether your fixed hours are still realistic. Adjust the plan there and then, rather than quietly abandoning it.

## What is the best way to study after a full day of work?

The best way is to match the task to your energy, and your method to how you will be assessed. Keep weekday evenings for shorter tasks, and leave your hardest thinking and writing for when you are fresh, even if that means an hour before work rather than an hour after it.

If the programme is assessed by written examination, practise writing answers under time rather than re-reading material. If it is assessed by assignments, start each one the week it is set and leave it open, rather than treating it as a single evening's work.
Re-reading feels productive and is the weakest use of the hours. Answering questions from memory is uncomfortable, and it is what actually works.

Use the commute and the dead time. Reading and lecture material work well in fragments: a bus ride, a lunch break, twenty minutes before bed. Save the continuous blocks for writing, which does not survive interruption. Students routinely cover most of their reading without ever sitting at a desk for it.

Use your work as well. Where assignments allow a real example, use one from your job, leaving out anything your employer would treat as confidential. It is faster to write, it is better than an invented case, and it makes the degree immediately useful rather than parallel to your working life.

## Should I tell my employer I am doing a degree?

Usually yes, unless your contract or workplace gives you a clear reason not to. Tell someone at home and someone at work that you are studying. The first protects your evenings, and most failures at this stage are domestic rather than academic. The second means a manager knows why you left at six on a Thursday. You are not asking permission, only removing the friction of explaining it every week.

There are exceptions where permission does matter. Government employees are generally expected to inform their department, and many need permission or a no-objection certificate, so see our guide for [Kerala government employees](/blog/degree-for-kerala-government-employees) before enrolling. In a private company, check your contract and HR policy for any rule on outside study, and ask about any support for fees or examination leave. Some employers have a scheme that nobody mentions until you ask.

At home, be specific. "I am studying on Tuesday and Thursday from eight to half past nine, and on Sunday mornings" gets much better cooperation than "I need more time to study". Where someone else will have to cover childcare or household work during those hours, agree that before the semester starts.

## What happens if I miss an exam or fall behind?

Falling behind is usually recoverable if you act early, though what you can do and what it costs depend on your university's rules. There will be a bad month: a project deadline, an illness, a wedding. Know in advance what the university's rules are for a deferred examination, a re-sit or a break in study, and what they cost. Knowing the mechanism exists is what makes a bad month survivable rather than terminal.

If you are going to miss something, contact your counsellor and the university that week, before the deadline rather than after. Requests for an extension or a rescheduled submission have a better chance when made in time; the gap between a problem and asking about it is what turns one paper into a lost semester.

> The people who finish are not the ones with more free time. They are the ones who asked for help in week three instead of week ten.

To restart after a lost month, do not try to catch up on everything. Start with the next assessment, since that is the one you can still influence. Then list the missed tasks and ask what can still be submitted, what can be sat later, and by when. Return to your fixed hours straight away, even at the minimum.

Also ask about the maximum period allowed to finish the programme. If a break is unavoidable, a planned break within those limits is better than simply disappearing.

## Choose a programme you can survive

Most of this is decided before you enrol, in the choice of examination pattern and schedule.
