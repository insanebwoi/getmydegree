The real cost of a distance or online degree is the total of tuition across all semesters, registration, examination fees and certificate charges, plus attestation if you will use it abroad, and the figure in an advertisement is often only the tuition. Fees in this sector are usually quoted as one number that is not the total, which is exactly why people get caught out later. Here is how the cost of a degree is actually built up, so you can compare any two offers on the same terms. We give no figures here, because they differ by university, programme and year. A counsellor gives you the full fee breakdown in writing.

## What is included in the total cost of a degree?

The total has five main lines, and the first is the one you are most likely to be quoted.

- **Tuition**, per semester or per year, multiplied by the number of semesters. This is the largest component.

- **Registration or enrolment**, usually charged once at admission.

- **Examination fees**, per semester, and the charge to re-sit a paper.

- **Certificates**: the degree certificate, consolidated marksheet and provisional certificate, and a migration certificate where you need one.

- **Attestation**, if the degree will be used abroad. Our guide to [degree attestation for the Gulf](/blog/degree-attestation-gulf) explains the process.

The figure in an advertisement is nearly always the first line only. Two programmes with identical tuition can differ substantially once examination and certification charges are added, and the cheaper tuition is sometimes the more expensive degree. Ask for all five lines in writing before you compare anything.

There are also costs that never appear on a fee sheet. A centre-based examination may mean travel, a stay and unpaid leave. A proctored examination may mean replacing an old laptop or paying for a better connection. These do not belong to the university, but they come out of your pocket, and for someone in the Gulf, flying home for a paper can outweigh a difference in tuition.

## How do I compare two degree fee quotes?

Compare two quotes by writing out the same lines for each and adding them up over the full duration, not by comparing the headline numbers. Here is a worked example with placeholders. Replace each letter with the figures from your own written quote.

| Line | Formula | Offer A | Offer B |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tuition | T × number of semesters | T1 × 6 | T2 × 6 |
| Registration | R, once | R1 | R2 |
| Examination | E × number of semesters | E1 × 6 | Included in T2? |
| Certificates | C, once | C1 | C2 |
| Attestation, if needed | A | A1 | A2 |

Suppose Offer A quotes a lower tuition, fee T1, and Offer B quotes a higher one, fee T2. If Offer B's tuition includes its examination fees and Offer A's does not, then T1 is not the number to compare. The number to compare is the sum of each column. Six semesters assumes a three-year programme with semester billing; for a two-year programme, or one billed annually, change the count to match your quote.

While you have the columns in front of you, also ask these:

- Is any line "to be announced" or "as per university norms"? If so, ask what it was charged last year, in writing, and mark it as uncertain.

- Do you have to pay anything further if a credit transfer reduces your papers? Credit transfer can change the fee, but do not assume a reduction until it is on paper. Our [credit transfer](/credit-transfer) page explains how the process works.

- Is anything a condition of enrolment but sold separately, such as study material or a platform charge?

## How do monthly instalments work for a degree?

Monthly instalments divide a fee into equal payments over an agreed period, and on our plans the total stays the same as paying up front.

Most universities already charge by semester, which is a form of instalment: you pay as you progress rather than up front. For many people that is enough.

Where a semester payment is still too large in one piece, our plans divide the fee into monthly payments. There is no interest and no processing fee, so the total you pay across the months equals the fee paid up front. The first instalment is due at enrolment and the rest fall on the same date each month. The schedule is agreed in writing at the start, not negotiated later.

In placeholder terms: if the agreed fee is fee X and it is spread over N months, each instalment is X divided by N, and N instalments add up to exactly X. If anyone's plan adds up to more than X, the difference is what the plan is costing you, whatever it is called.

When you compare any instalment arrangement, ours or anyone else's, check three things: the total you will have paid at the end, whether that is higher than paying in full, and what happens if you miss a payment.

## Is a degree EMI the same as an education loan?

No, not always, and the difference matters. "EMI" is used for several quite different arrangements:

| Arrangement | Who you owe | What to check |
|---|---|---|
| University's semester payment | The university | Fee revisions, late charges |
| Provider's monthly instalment plan | The provider, as agreed in writing | Total paid, missed-payment terms |
| EMI through a bank or finance company | The lender | Interest, processing fee, your credit record |

The third kind is a loan, even when it is advertised as an easy monthly payment. You sign an agreement with a lender, and that lender's terms apply to you. If you withdraw from the programme, you may still owe the lender. A missed payment may affect your credit record, which can matter later for a home or vehicle loan.

Be careful with "no-cost EMI" in particular. Ask who the lender is, whether a processing fee applies, and whether the course fee was raised or a discount withdrawn to pay for it. Read the loan agreement before you sign it, not only the sales leaflet.

## What should I ask before paying a degree fee?

Ask how the fee could change, and who receives your money. These are questions worth asking any provider:

- Is the fee fixed for the full duration, or revised each year?

- What happens if I take a break in study for a semester?

- What is refundable, and by when, if I withdraw early?

- Is the fee paid to the university directly, and will I get the university's receipt?

That last one is a safety question more than a financial one. A fee paid to a study centre in its own name, with no receipt from the university, is a common feature of the losses we hear about. Our guide to [red flags before enrolling](/blog/red-flags-before-enrolling-in-an-online-degree) covers others.

Do not let the fee discussion replace the validity check. A low fee is worth nothing if the degree cannot be used. Validity depends on the university's recognition, the programme's [DEB](https://deb.ugc.ac.in/) entitlement for your admission year, and the wording of the job notification you have in mind.

We do not take payment before you have seen the university name, the programme, its DEB entitlement and the full fee in writing. If any provider asks you to pay to "block a seat" before showing you those, treat it as a warning rather than urgency.

## Why should I keep my fee receipts?

Keep them because fee records are asked for at unexpected moments, including at graduation and sometimes years later during verification. Keep every receipt with your enrolment number, in one folder, with a scanned copy somewhere safe. Keep the written fee breakdown and instalment schedule with them, since they prove what you were told and when.
