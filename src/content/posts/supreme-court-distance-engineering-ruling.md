On 3 November 2017, the Supreme Court of India decided *Orissa Lift Irrigation Corp. Ltd. v. Rabi Sankar Patro*. It held that engineering degrees awarded through distance education by deemed universities without AICTE approval were not valid, and that technical education by distance mode could not continue without AICTE approval.

This is the judgment people mean when they search "Supreme Court distance B.Tech judgement". It is the main reason counsellors in Kerala, including us, tell every diploma holder the same thing: a distance or online B.Tech is not a route to a recognised engineering degree. Below is what the case was about, what the court ordered, and what it means for someone deciding today.

## What did the Supreme Court decide in 2017?

The court held that the AICTE Regulations of 1994 apply to deemed universities, so a deemed university could not start a technical course, including engineering, without AICTE approval. Because the deemed universities in the case had run B.Tech programmes by distance mode without that approval, the degrees they awarded did not stand.

The court also said that whether engineering can be taught by distance education is a question for AICTE to decide, not for a university on its own. In the absence of AICTE guidelines expressly permitting it, the universities were not justified in offering engineering degrees by distance.

## How did the case begin?

It started with a promotion dispute in Odisha. According to the judgment, Rabi Sankar Patro held a diploma in electrical engineering and worked as a Junior Engineer with the Orissa Lift Irrigation Corporation. While in service, he obtained a B.Tech (Civil) through distance education in 2009 from a deemed university in Udaipur, Rajasthan, and claimed promotion as an in-service graduate engineer.

The corporation refused, arguing that the degree was not recognised because the university had no permission to run engineering by distance. The Orissa High Court sided with the employee. Separately, the Punjab and Haryana High Court, hearing a public interest petition, held that degrees of this kind were illegal. The dispute reached the Supreme Court along with related cases on the same question.

The story will sound familiar to many Kerala readers. A working diploma holder, a promotion that needs a B.Tech, and a distance degree that seemed to solve the problem. That is exactly the situation the judgment warns against.

## Which universities were involved?

The judgment dealt with four deemed universities, which it refers to by short names:

- **JRN**, JRN Rajasthan Vidyapeeth, Udaipur, Rajasthan
- **IASE**, Sardarshahr, Rajasthan
- **AAI**, Allahabad, Uttar Pradesh
- **VMRF**, Vinayaka Mission's Research Foundation, Salem, Tamil Nadu

The ruling is not limited to these four in its effect. Its reasoning, that technical education needs AICTE approval and cannot be offered by distance without it, applies to every institution.

## What did the court order for students who already had these degrees?

The court treated students in two groups, depending on when they enrolled.

**Students admitted in the academic sessions 2001 to 2005.** The UGC had granted these batches ex post facto approval. The court set that approval aside and suspended their engineering degrees. It then gave them a way out:

- AICTE was to devise a test for them, and students had to opt for it by 15 January 2018;
- each student would get no more than two chances to pass;
- those who passed would have their degrees revived and their benefits restored;
- those who failed, or chose not to take the test, would lose the degree, with those who declined entitled to a refund of fees.

**Students admitted after the 2001-2005 sessions.** Their distance engineering degrees were recalled and treated as cancelled, and all benefits secured on the basis of them were withdrawn. The universities were directed to refund the fees they had collected by 31 May 2018.

These are hard outcomes. People who had studied, paid and in some cases been promoted lost the qualification years later. That is the real risk of a degree in a mode the regulators do not permit: the problem may not surface for years, and when it does, the job or promotion built on it can go too.

## What else did the court direct?

The judgment went beyond the individual degrees. In brief, the court:

- directed the CBI to investigate the officials who granted permissions contrary to policy, and the institutions alleged to have misused their position for commercial gain;
- asked the UGC to consider whether the deemed university status of the four institutions should be withdrawn;
- restrained all deemed universities, from the 2018-19 academic session, from running courses by distance education unless permitted and approved course by course, with study centres individually inspected;
- suggested that the Union government consider a committee of eminent persons to recommend an oversight and regulatory framework for this area.

For a reader today, the important part is the principle: an engineering degree by distance, without AICTE approval, is not a valid degree, however official the certificate looks.

## Is a distance B.Tech valid after the 2017 judgment?

No. The 2017 judgment was followed by the UGC (Open and Distance Learning Programmes and Online Programmes) Regulations, 2020, which list engineering among the programmes that universities may not offer in distance or online mode. The regulations were amended in 2021 and 2022, and engineering is still excluded.

So today the position is settled from both sides: the court held that distance engineering degrees without AICTE approval were invalid, and the regulations do not allow engineering in distance or online mode at all. ## Does the ruling affect evening or working-professional B.Tech programmes?

No. The ruling was about engineering by distance education. A B.Tech for working professionals is a regular, in-person programme at an AICTE-approved institution, with classes in the evening or at other flexible times, including weekends and holidays. AICTE has allowed this format from 2023-24 onwards.

In Kerala, KTU offers B.Tech for working professionals at AICTE-approved institutions from 2024-25. Students join the second year (third semester) after qualifying the Lateral Entry Test conducted by LBS Centre, and the programme takes not less than three years. ## What should I do if I hold a distance B.Tech?

Be honest with yourself about where it stands, and do not keep building on it. Some practical steps:

- **Find out exactly what you hold.** Note the university, the mode printed on your marksheets, and the years of enrolment and award.
- **Do not use it for new applications that ask for a B.Tech.** Certificates are checked at recruitment, at appointment and again at promotion.
- **Take advice on your service position.** If the degree was used for a promotion, speak to your service association or a lawyer about your specific situation; the outcome can depend on rules and dates we cannot judge from outside.
- **Look at a valid route now.** If you have a diploma and are working, the working-professional B.Tech or regular [lateral entry through the LET](/blog/kerala-btech-lateral-entry-let) may still be open to you.

If an agent tells you a distance B.Tech can be "regularised" for a fee, treat that as a warning sign, not a solution. Our list of [red flags before enrolling in an online degree](/blog/red-flags-before-enrolling-in-an-online-degree) helps you spot these offers.
