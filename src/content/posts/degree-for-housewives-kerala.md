Yes, you can finish or start a degree after years spent running a home, and a recognized distance or online programme is built for exactly this: you study from home, on a schedule you set, and sit examinations at set times. If you studied at college before marriage or children, some of that work may still count. What matters is choosing a properly recognized programme, being honest about the hours you have, and deciding what you want the degree to do for you.

## You are not starting from zero

Many of the women who come to us apologise for the gap before they have finished their first sentence. There is no need. Managing a household budget, coordinating school schedules, looking after elderly parents or helping in a family business are real skills, and they show up in how people study. Returning students are often more organized and more focused than they were at eighteen, because they know exactly why they are there.

It is also worth saying plainly: the decision to come back is yours to make, for your own reasons. Some women return because the family needs a second income. Some return because the children are older and the days have room in them again. Some simply want the certificate they were one year away from. All of these are good reasons, and none of them needs to be defended to anyone.

The gap itself is not a problem for admission. Universities do not ask why you stopped. They ask what you have completed.

## If you studied before, check what still counts

A large number of homemakers left a degree part-way, after the first or second year, often when they married or moved. That study may not be lost.

- **Look for your old marksheets.** Even if you only have some of them, bring what you have. Universities can usually issue duplicates of the rest.

- **Ask for a credit assessment.** Under current UGC rules, credits from a recognized university can in many cases be carried to another, subject to the receiving university's rules. Our page on [credit transfer](/credit-transfer) explains how this works.

- **Do not assume it is too old.** Each university sets its own limits, and an assessment is the only way to find out.

If you completed your 12th and never went to college, you start the first year of a three-year degree. That is the most common starting point of all. Our guide to [returning to study after a long gap](/blog/degree-after-long-gap) covers the first few months.

## Deciding what the degree is for

This is worth thinking about before choosing a subject, because the answer shapes everything else.

- **Returning to paid work.** If you plan to apply for office, accounts or administrative jobs, a B.Com or BBA is the usual choice.

- **Government jobs.** Many Kerala PSC posts ask for a degree in any discipline. A recognized distance degree is generally accepted, but some posts require a regular course of study. Read our article on [distance degrees for Kerala government jobs](/blog/government-jobs-distance-degree-kerala) before you pick.

- **Teaching, counselling or social work.** A BA is a common first step, and an MSW at postgraduate level leads towards social work roles. Teaching posts usually need further qualifications such as a B.Ed.

- **A home business.** Tailoring, catering, tuition or online selling all benefit from the accounts and management basics in a B.Com or BBA.

- **For yourself.** Finishing what you started is a good enough reason. You do not need to justify it.

## Choosing a programme that fits family life

Distance and online programmes look similar on a brochure but work differently in practice.

- **Examination pattern.** Some universities hold written examinations at centres, which means a day or two away from home each term. Others use assignments or proctored online examinations from home. Choose the one you can actually manage.

- **Travel to examination centres.** If written examinations are held at centres, ask where they are and how far you would need to travel. A centre an hour away is very different from one across the state, especially with young children at home.

- **Study material.** Printed books suit some people better than screens, especially if the family shares one phone or laptop.

- **Calendar.** Find out when examinations fall. If they clash with a major family commitment every year, that is worth knowing now.

Our explainer on [online versus distance modes](/blog/online-vs-distance-degree-modes) sets out the differences.

## Recognition matters more than speed

Before paying anyone, confirm that the university is recognized by the UGC and that the specific programme has UGC Distance Education Bureau entitlement for the year you are admitted. A degree that cannot pass a background check is of no use when you apply for a job.

Be wary of anyone offering a degree in one year, a certificate dated to a year you did not study, or a promise that you can skip the examinations. None of these are legitimate.

## Making the hours work

Most programmes need around six to ten hours a week. That sounds like a lot until you break it down.

- **Find your fixed slots.** Early morning before the house wakes, the hour after children leave for school, or after dinner. Pick two or three and keep them.

- **Use small gaps for reading.** Ten minutes while rice cooks is enough to read a few pages. Save longer blocks for writing answers.

- **Tell the family.** The evenings you need are easier to protect when everyone at home knows why. Many women find their children become their most reliable supporters once they see a parent studying.

- **Plan for the difficult month.** Illness, a wedding, a festival. Know the university's rules on deferred examinations before you need them.

Our guide to [studying while working full time](/blog/studying-while-working-full-time) applies just as well to running a home.

## What it costs

Many women tell us the fee is the part they find hardest to raise at home, more than the study itself. It helps to know the full figure before that conversation, not after. Fees depend on the university and the programme. A counsellor gives you the complete breakdown in writing before you decide, covering tuition, registration, examination and certificate charges. Monthly instalments with no interest and no processing fee are available, which suits a household budget better than a single large payment.

## Next steps

- Gather your 10th and 12th certificates, and any college marksheets you have.

- Think about what you want the degree to lead to, even roughly.

- Visit or call for an assessment. We check what your earlier study is worth, suggest programmes that fit your time, and show you the university name, the programme's DEB entitlement and the full fee in writing before any payment.

- You are welcome to bring your spouse, a parent or a friend to the meeting. Many people prefer to, and the questions they ask are usually good ones.
