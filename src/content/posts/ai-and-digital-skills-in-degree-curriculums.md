To judge whether a degree really teaches AI or digital skills, ignore the programme name and read the syllabus: look for named papers with credits, practical work you are assessed on, and a recent revision date. Even a good syllabus only gets you started. A degree can give you the foundations and a credential, but it will not keep you current on tools or give you a portfolio, and you will need to build those yourself.

Nearly every university now advertises something with artificial intelligence or data in the name. Some of it is a genuine addition to the curriculum. Some of it is the same syllabus with a new cover. Telling them apart is not difficult, and this article shows how. We are not assessing any particular university here. Syllabi change, so ask for the current one for your admission year.

## How do I know if a degree syllabus really covers AI?

Ask for the paper list, not the programme brochure. A genuine specialisation shows up as named papers with credit weightings attached: statistics and data analysis, database systems, an applied analytics paper, a project. If the only evidence is a line in the marketing copy and the paper list is unchanged from the ordinary programme, the specialisation exists in the brochure.

Then read each relevant paper's unit list, and look for these things:

- **Weight.** How many credits does the paper carry compared with the rest of the programme? One small paper is an introduction, not a skill.

- **Prerequisites and order.** Serious work in this area builds up: spreadsheets and basic statistics first, then databases or programming, then analysis or machine learning. A single paper that promises all of these will be shallow.

- **Practical work.** Look for lab sessions, datasets, or a tool you actually use. Ask how a distance or online student does this practical work, and on what software.

- **Assessment.** Does the assessment involve producing anything? A specialisation assessed entirely through written examination teaches you to describe the tools. One with a project makes you use them, which gives you something to show.

- **Date.** Ask when the syllabus was last revised. An AI paper written several years ago may be sound on principles and out of date on tools. That is fine if the units are about principles, and a problem if they promise current tools.

| What the brochure says | What to look for in the syllabus | Warning sign |
|---|---|---|
| "AI-powered curriculum" | Named papers on data or AI, with credits | No change in the paper list |
| "Industry-ready skills" | Project or lab work that is assessed | Written examination only |
| "Learn the latest tools" | Named tools, and how you get access | No tool named anywhere |
| "Specialisation in analytics" | Several linked papers, not just one | One elective with a new title |

An invented example of a thin paper: "AI for Managers", a small elective whose units are the history of AI, types of machine learning, ethics, and case studies, assessed by a written paper. It can be a decent introduction, but you would leave able to discuss AI, not to work with it. Compare that with a set of linked papers on statistics, databases and analytics, ending in a project on a real dataset. Same marketing line, very different result.

Whether the specialisation appears on your marksheet or degree is a separate question, covered in our article on [micro-credentials and specialisations](/blog/micro-credentials-and-specializations).

## Which degree is best for learning AI and digital skills?

There is no single best degree, because how far the syllabus can go depends on the base programme and on what you plan to do with it.

- **In commerce and management**, such as a [BBA](/courses/bba) or MBA, analytics electives connect to work many people already do in spreadsheets. If you handle sales, accounts or stock data, you can often apply what you learn the same week.

- **In science and computing**, such as a [BCA](/courses/bca) or MCA, the base programme normally includes programming, so additions in data or AI have something to build on. Our comparison of [B.Sc and BCA for tech jobs](/blog/bsc-vs-bca-for-tech-jobs) goes further on that choice.

- **In general arts programmes**, a digital marketing or applied analytics elective, where one is offered, can add a practical skill that the rest of the degree may not.

It should not override the practical factors, though. A programme with an excellent analytics track and examinations you cannot physically attend is the wrong programme. Pick the recognized programme that fits your schedule first, then, among the options that survive, prefer the one whose electives point where you are going. Check the university's recognition and the programme's DEB entitlement for your admission year in the usual way.

## What will a degree not teach you about AI?

A degree will not teach you the things that change quickly or are learnt only by doing. Be realistic about these, whichever university you choose:

- **Current tools.** AI tools change faster than any syllabus can be revised and approved. Expect the degree to teach you principles, and to learn the tools separately.

- **A portfolio.** A marksheet shows that you passed. Employers hiring for practical skills often want to see work. A small analysis you did, a dashboard you built, or a project on a public dataset can do that, and you will usually have to choose to make it yourself.

- **Judgement about AI output.** Knowing when an answer from an AI tool is wrong needs knowledge of your own field. This is one reason the ordinary subject matter of your degree still counts: an accountant can spot a wrong tax treatment that a beginner would miss.

- **Daily practice.** A few hours for an assignment will not build fluency. A small task from your own job, done regularly, will.

An elective inside a degree is also not a substitute for a specialist qualification, and nobody should choose a university on this basis alone. The degree is the credential. The elective is a useful line underneath it.

## Will an AI elective get me a job?

No elective can do that by itself, and any provider who suggests otherwise is selling you something. What it can do is give you a reason to be taken seriously, and something to talk about, for roles that already use data. Where the degree itself is a requirement, as in many Kerala government posts and in corporate promotion rules, the recruiter will look at whether you hold the qualification named, with the validity checks that apply: recognition, DEB entitlement for your admission year, and the notification's wording. An elective's title will rarely change that outcome.

A practical plan for a working person is to combine the two. Use the degree for the credential and the foundations. Alongside it, learn one tool in depth through short courses, including government-run platforms such as SWAYAM, and try it on a real task. Keep a short record of what you did and what changed. That record often says more in an interview than an elective's name.

## Questions worth asking before you enrol

- Which named papers make up the specialisation, and what are their credits?

- Is there a project or capstone, and is it assessed?

- How do distance or online students get access to software and practical work?

- When was this syllabus last revised, and which version applies to my admission year?

- Does the final marksheet and degree name the specialisation, or only the base degree?
